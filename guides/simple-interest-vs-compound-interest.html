Simple Interest vs Compound Interest

UC Editorial Team
Published 2026-02-10 6 min read

Simple interest and compound interest answer the same basic question — how much will interest add to a balance — but they get there in fundamentally different ways. The distinction comes down to one question: does interest earn interest?

Simple interest

Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal, every single period, using:

Interest = P × r × t

The interest earned each year is identical, because it's always based on the same starting principal. A $10,000 loan at 5% simple interest earns exactly $500 a year, every year, for as long as the loan runs.

Compound interest

Compound interest is calculated on the principal plus any interest already added. Each period's interest is a little larger than the last, because the balance it's calculated on has grown. Over time this produces a curve rather than a straight line — slow at first, then accelerating.

Side-by-side over 20 years

Take $10,000 at 5% annually for 20 years under each method:

MethodInterest after 20 yearsFinal balance
Simple interest$10,000.00$20,000.00
Compound interest (annual)$16,532.98$26,532.98

Both start from the same principal and rate. The compounding method produces over $6,500 more, purely because each year's interest becomes part of the base for the next year's calculation.

As a borrower, simple interest is usually cheaper over time. As a saver or investor, compound interest works in your favor — the same mechanism, opposite outcome, depending on which side of the transaction you're on.

Which one applies to you?

Most everyday savings accounts, mortgages, and investment accounts use compound interest. Simple interest shows up more often in short-term loans, some auto loans, and certain bonds. When comparing two financial products, checking which method is used — and how often it compounds — is one of the most useful things you can do before signing anything.

Frequently Asked Questions

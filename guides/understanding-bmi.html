Understanding BMI

UC Editorial Team
Published 2026-02-25 6 min read

Body Mass Index (BMI) is a simple screening measure that relates your weight to your height. It's widely used because it's easy to calculate from just two numbers — but it's important to understand both what it measures and what it doesn't.

The formula

BMI = Weight (kg) / Height (m)²

In imperial units: BMI = (Weight in pounds / Height in inches²) × 703.

Worked example

A person weighing 70 kg and standing 1.75 m tall:

StepCalculationResult
Height squared1.75²3.0625 m²
BMI70 / 3.062522.9

The standard BMI categories

CategoryBMI range
UnderweightBelow 18.5
Healthy weight18.5 – 24.9
Overweight25.0 – 29.9
Obesity30.0 and above

These ranges come from the World Health Organization's widely used classification for adults.

What BMI doesn't measure

BMI uses only weight and height — it can't distinguish muscle mass from fat mass, doesn't account for where fat is distributed on the body, and the standard adult ranges aren't designed for children, pregnant people, or highly muscular athletes, who can register as "overweight" by BMI despite having low body fat.

BMI is a population-level screening tool, not a diagnostic one. It's useful as a quick, consistent starting point, but it should be considered alongside other measures — and a healthcare professional's assessment — rather than treated as a complete picture of health on its own.

Why BMI remains widely used

Despite its limitations, BMI is inexpensive to calculate, requires no special equipment, and correlates reasonably well with health risk across large populations — which is why it remains a standard first-pass screening measure in clinical and public health settings, even as more detailed measures (waist circumference, body fat percentage, waist-to-hip ratio) are used alongside it for a fuller picture.

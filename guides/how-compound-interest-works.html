How Compound Interest Works

UC Editorial Team
Published 2026-02-03 · Updated 2026-06-14 7 min read

Compound interest is interest calculated not just on the money you originally put in (the principal), but also on the interest that money has already earned. That single idea — interest earning interest — is why compounding is often described as one of the most powerful forces in personal finance.

The formula

The standard compound interest formula is:

A = P × (1 + r/n)n×t

Where A is the final amount, P is the principal, r is the annual interest rate (as a decimal), n is how many times per year interest compounds, and t is the number of years.

A worked example

Suppose you invest $5,000 at an annual rate of 6%, compounded monthly, for 10 years. Here, P = 5000, r = 0.06, n = 12, t = 10.

VariableValue
Principal (P)$5,000
Rate (r)6%
Compounds per year (n)12
Years (t)10
Final amount (A)$9,096.98

That's $4,096.98 in growth on a $5,000 principal — more than 80% growth, without adding another dollar. Compare that to simple interest at the same rate over the same period, which would earn exactly $3,000 in interest, no more.

The gap between simple and compound interest widens with time. Over 10 years the difference above is roughly $1,097 — over 30 years at the same rate, it grows to tens of thousands of dollars, purely from interest compounding on interest.

Why the compounding frequency matters

The more frequently interest compounds — annually, monthly, daily — the faster the balance grows, because each compounding period locks in a small amount of extra interest that then itself earns interest. The difference between monthly and daily compounding is usually small in percentage terms, but the difference between annual and monthly compounding can be meaningful over long time horizons.

Where you'll see compound interest

Compound interest works in your favor in savings accounts, certificates of deposit, and investment accounts. It works against you in the same way on credit card balances and some loans, where unpaid interest is added to the balance and then itself accrues interest — which is exactly why carrying a balance on high-interest debt compounds so quickly.

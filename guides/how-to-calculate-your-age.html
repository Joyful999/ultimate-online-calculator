How to Calculate Your Age

UC Editorial Team
Published 2026-01-29 4 min read

Calculating age sounds trivial until you actually try to do it precisely — leap years, differing month lengths, and the exact "has the birthday happened yet this year" question all complicate what looks like simple subtraction.

The basic method

Start with the difference in years between today's date and the birth date. Then adjust: if today's month and day haven't yet reached the birth month and day, subtract one year, because the most recent birthday hasn't happened yet this year.

Worked example

Someone born on 14 September 2000, and today is 25 August 2026.

StepDetail
Raw year difference2026 − 2000 = 26
Has this year's birthday happened?No — 25 August comes before 14 September
Adjustment26 − 1 = 25
Exact age25 years

To get the extra months and days: count from the last birthday (14 September 2025) to today (25 August 2026) — that's 11 months and 11 days. So the full precise age is 25 years, 11 months, 11 days.

Why leap years complicate this

Someone born on 29 February only has an actual birthday once every four years. Most age calculators treat their birthday as 28 February (or 1 March) in non-leap years for the purpose of counting a birthday as "having happened" — but the underlying day-count math still needs to correctly account for every leap year that occurred between the birth date and today, or the day-count will drift by one day per leap year missed.

Calculating age in total days

For some purposes — insurance, eligibility rules, some legal contexts — age needs to be expressed as a total day count rather than years/months/days. This is done by counting the exact number of calendar days between the birth date and today, which automatically and correctly accounts for every leap year in between, since it's just counting real calendar days rather than approximating months.

If you need age for an official or legal purpose (school enrollment cut-offs, insurance eligibility, retirement age), always confirm against the exact rule used by that institution — some use "age as of December 31" or similar conventions rather than your literal current age.
